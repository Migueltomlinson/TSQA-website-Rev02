Most of the time, sending someone out on their own is completely normal. A fitter goes to a client site alone. A technician does a callout after hours. A contractor finishes a job while everyone else has gone home. None of that is illegal, and for a lot of small businesses it is simply how the work gets done.

The problem starts when "he knows what he is doing" is the only control you have.

## What happened

On 9 September 2026, WorkSafe published the outcome of a prosecution following the death of Tejay Alan Debenham, a forestry worker who was felling trees at a rural property near Dannevirke in November 2023. He was working alone and unsupervised when a tree limb struck him and he fell from a nearby cliff. The site had large wind-affected trees, suspended and interlocked branches, restricted escape routes, and trees growing close to an eroded cliff edge.

His employer, Trevor Beale, was charged under sections 36(1)(a), 48(1) and 48(2)(b) of the Health and Safety at Work Act 2015. In the Dannevirke District Court he was ordered to pay $254,740 in reparation to the family, $1,000 in legal costs, and a $5,000 fine.

WorkSafe's Central Region Manager, Nigel Formosa, made the point that the lessons go well beyond forestry. One line in particular is worth pinning above your desk: taking someone's word for their training or experience is not enough.

## The four steps that were missing

The prosecution identified four reasonably practicable steps that were not taken. Read them as a checklist, because that is effectively what they are:

1. Confirming the worker's experience, training and qualifications.
2. Completing an adequate induction.
3. Ensuring appropriate supervision.
4. Putting an adequate check-in and check-out procedure in place.

None of these are expensive. None of them require a system built for a company ten times your size. What they require is evidence that the step actually happened.

That is the gap I see most often in small engineering, fabrication and contracting businesses. The knowledge is there. The judgement is there. The record is not. And when something goes wrong, an inspector cannot assess what you knew, only what you can show.

## Why this matters more, not less, from April 2027

The Health and Safety at Work Amendment Act 2026 received Royal Assent on 9 July 2026 and comes into force on 1 April 2027. It narrows the duties of small PCBUs, defined as businesses with fewer than 20 employees, so they focus on critical risks. A critical risk is one associated with a hazard likely to cause death, a notifiable injury or illness, a notifiable incident, or an occupational disease.

Some business owners have read that as a signal to relax. It is not. A worker operating alone around falling objects, moving plant, height, confined spaces, live electricity or hot work sits squarely inside the definition of critical risk. The reform trims the paperwork around the low-consequence end. It does nothing to reduce what you owe the person who could be killed.

## What to do this week

Pick your highest-risk task that someone performs alone or with minimal supervision. Then work through four questions honestly.

**Can you produce the evidence of competence?** Not a belief, a document. A ticket, a unit standard, a signed verification of competence by someone qualified to judge it, or a record of observed assessment. If a worker's only qualification is that they have done it for years, write down who observed them, on what task, and when.

**Was the induction structured and checked?** WorkSafe's expectation is that an induction is documented and understanding is confirmed, with clear task limits. A five minute conversation in the ute does not meet that bar.

**Is the supervision arrangement real?** The point WorkSafe makes here is blunt. High-risk work must not go ahead just because the usual supervisor is unavailable. If your system depends on one person being there, write down what happens when they are not.

**Does your check-in and check-out system trigger something?** A procedure that says "the worker will call if there is a problem" is not a system. It needs a nominated person responsible for monitoring, an agreed interval that matches the risk, and a defined action if contact is missed. Confirm the arrangement before the work starts rather than assuming it.

There is a commercial angle too. Competency records and lone worker procedures are standard evidence in SiteWise, Tōtika, IMPAC PREQUAL and SHE assessments, and in ISO 45001 audits. Fixing this properly protects your people and strengthens the file you hand to clients.
