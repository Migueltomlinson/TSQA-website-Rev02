You can see a fall from height. You can see an unguarded machine. You cannot see the thing that kills more New Zealand workers than either of them.

WorkSafe estimates that between 750 and 900 New Zealanders die each year from work-related health causes, and that up to 79 percent of those deaths are linked to airborne substances. Dust, vapours, gases and fumes. The exposure happens quietly over years, the diagnosis arrives long after the job finished, and almost nobody connects the two at the time.

On 23 July 2026, WorkSafe released a new set of plain-language resources on workplace exposure standards, or WES. The timing matters, and not just because dust is finally getting attention.

## What WorkSafe actually released

The new material includes educational videos, practical case studies, and a guide to working with occupational hygienists. WorkSafe has also updated its inspector training so frontline messaging is consistent.

Two things in the release are worth reading carefully.

First, WorkSafe has been clear about what WES are and are not. They are guideline values that help you assess exposure risk. They are not compulsory limits, they are not set in regulation, and they are not designed to be applied by a business on its own. Your duty under the Health and Safety at Work Act 2015 is to eliminate or minimise exposure so far as is reasonably practicable. A WES number is a tool that helps you judge whether you have done that. It is not a permission slip to sit just under the line.

Second, WorkSafe has confirmed WES values for flour dust, softwood dust, hardwood dust and welding fumes. These are no longer interim. If you run a joinery, a bakery, a fabrication shop or an engineering workshop anywhere from Tauranga to New Plymouth, those four cover a lot of what your people breathe every day.

## Why this collides with the law change

The Health and Safety at Work Amendment Act 2026 received Royal Assent on 9 July 2026 and comes into force on 1 April 2027. It creates a new category of small PCBU, being a business with fewer than 20 workers, and requires small PCBUs to comply with core HSWA duties in relation to critical risks, alongside existing welfare obligations.

Critical risks are those associated with hazards likely to cause death, serious injury, serious illness or long-term harm.

Read that definition again with dust in mind. Silicosis is long-term harm. Occupational asthma from wood dust or flour dust is long-term harm. Welding fume exposure is linked to lung cancer. If you are a small PCBU planning to narrow your focus to critical risks from April 2027, airborne exposure belongs on that list. Plenty of businesses are quietly assuming the reform means less work. For dust and fume risk, it may well mean more scrutiny, not less, because the reform pushes everyone toward the risks that actually kill people rather than the paperwork that does not.

## What to do about it

You do not need a hygienist on retainer to make real progress. Start here.

**Write down where the dust and fumes actually come from.** Walk the site and list the tasks, not the areas. Dry cutting concrete. Sanding MDF. MIG welding in a corner with no extraction. Sweeping up at the end of the day. Task-level detail is what makes the rest of this work.

**Fix it at the source before you reach for masks.** Water suppression, on-tool extraction, local exhaust ventilation and enclosure all beat respiratory protective equipment, because they work whether or not the person wearing the mask shaved that morning. Under the HSWA hierarchy, PPE is the last control, not the first.

**If you rely on RPE, do it properly.** Right class of filter, fit tested to the individual, clean-shaven face seal, stored somewhere that is not the ute floor, and replaced on a schedule. An unfit-tested P2 mask is a comfort item, not a control.

**Know when you have crossed into monitoring territory.** The Health and Safety at Work (Hazardous Substances) Regulations 2017 bring exposure monitoring and health monitoring duties into play in certain situations. If you are unsure whether they apply to you, that uncertainty is itself the finding, and it is worth resolving before an inspector does it for you.

**Record it.** Exposure and health monitoring records are health information, so the Privacy Act 2020 applies. Store them securely, control who can see them, and keep them for the retention periods that apply. Getting this wrong creates a second problem on top of the first.

## The cost of leaving it

There is no invoice for doing nothing on dust, which is exactly why it gets left. The bill turns up later as a WorkSafe investigation, an ACC claim, a worker who cannot do the work anymore, or a pre-qualification assessment where you cannot show how you manage a risk your own industry is known for. IMPAC PREQUAL, SiteWise, SHE and Tōtika assessors are increasingly asking about occupational health, not just safety. A thin answer there costs you contracts.
