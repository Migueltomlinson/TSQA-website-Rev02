An Auckland construction company was fined $38,500 last month for a mistake that took about five minutes to make.

WorkSafe issued Grace Motors Limited, trading as Grace Construction, a prohibition notice on 30 August 2024 after an inspector saw workers on the second floor of a residential build in Kelston, working near an unprotected edge. The site also had temporary platforms, partially removed scaffolding, and ladders that were neither compliant nor secured.

The notice said stop work at height on level two. Inspectors came back on 2 September and saw workers at height. They came back again on 4 September and saw the same thing. The company was sentenced in the Waitakere District Court on 20 August 2026 and fined $38,500 plus costs, under sections 107(1) and 107(2)(b) of the Health and Safety at Work Act 2015.

Nobody was hurt. That is the point worth sitting with. There was no incident, no injury, no ACC claim. The fine was for ignoring the notice.

## Improvement notice or prohibition notice, and why the difference matters

These two things get treated as the same piece of paper on a lot of sites, and they are not.

An **improvement notice** says there is a problem, here is what you need to fix, and here is how long you have. You keep working while you fix it.

A **prohibition notice** says stop, now. The inspector has decided there is a serious risk of death or serious injury and that activity does not restart until the problem is fixed and the notice is lifted. There is no grace period and no partial compliance.

Under HSWA, failing to comply with a prohibition notice carries a maximum fine of $500,000 for a company and $100,000 for an individual, including a self-employed PCBU. Actual sentences in recent cases have run from roughly $23,000 to $180,000. That is money paid for a paperwork failure rather than an accident.

## What to do in the first 48 hours

**Read the notice out loud with your site supervisor.** Most breaches are not defiance. They are someone assuming the notice covered one corner of the site, or one crew, or expired when the inspector drove off. A prohibition notice covers exactly what it says and nothing less. If the wording is unclear, ring the inspector and ask. That call is free and it is not an admission of anything.

**Physically stop the work.** Not a toolbox mention. Barricade the area, take the ladders away, lock the plant, and tell every subcontractor on site, not just your own people. You are the PCBU. If a subbie's crew works through your notice, that is still your problem.

**Write down what you did and when.** Date, time, who you told, what you put in place. This record is the difference between a fixed problem and an argument you cannot win later.

**Fix the actual hazard, not the appearance of it.** Edge protection that meets the standard. Scaffolding signed off. Ladders secured and rated. Then contact WorkSafe, tell them what has changed, and ask for the notice to be lifted before anyone goes back up.

**Ask why it happened.** An inspector issuing a prohibition notice means a critical risk got past your system. Something in your risk assessment, your induction, or your subcontractor management did not work. Find it now, while the answer is obvious.

## The cost you do not see on the fine sheet

Enforcement notices follow you. Most New Zealand pre-qualification schemes, including SiteWise, Tōtika, IMPAC PREQUAL and SHE, ask you to declare enforcement action and notifiable events. A prohibition notice you handled well and closed out is a manageable conversation with a prospective client. A conviction for ignoring one is a different conversation, and on some tender lists it ends the conversation.

There is also timing to think about. The Health and Safety at Work Amendment Act 2026 received Royal Assent on 9 July 2026 and comes into force on 1 April 2027. It narrows what smaller businesses have to manage, with a formal definition of critical risk and a lighter set of duties for a small PCBU with fewer than 20 workers. Some operators have read that as a signal to ease off. Read it the other way. Working at height with no edge protection is a textbook critical risk, and critical risk is precisely what the new law tells you to concentrate on. The obligations that remain are the ones with the sharpest teeth.

## The quiet version of this problem

Most businesses reading this have never had a notice and assume they never will. The Grace Construction crew probably thought the same thing on 29 August. The honest question is not whether you would receive a notice. It is whether, if one arrived on a Friday afternoon with two subcontractors on site and your supervisor on leave, your system would actually stop the work.

If you are not confident about the answer, that is worth a conversation before an inspector asks it for you.
