Ask ten tradespeople in the Bay of Plenty what the height rule is and most will tell you the same thing: you need fall protection above three metres. It is one of the most persistent myths in New Zealand construction, and WorkSafe has just spent an entire guidance rewrite pulling it apart.

## What has actually changed

In August 2026 WorkSafe reissued its guidance on working safely at height. The headline message is blunt. There is no height below which a fall risk can be ignored. The duty under the Health and Safety at Work Act 2015 has always been to manage the risk of a fall that could injure someone, not to measure a distance and decide whether the rules switch on.

The three metre figure came from old Health and Safety in Employment Act material and was never a threshold for when duties began. WorkSafe guidance at the time even warned people they were reading it wrong. Two decades later the misreading is still on job sites, still in method statements, and still turning up in safety plans we review.

The data explains why WorkSafe keeps pushing on this. Around 70 percent of falls are from ladders and roofs, most commonly on residential sites, and more than half of injury falls are from under three metres. A fall from a stepladder onto a concrete slab does not care what your policy says the threshold is.

## Why the timing matters

This lands eight months before the Health and Safety at Work Amendment Act 2026 comes into force on 1 April 2027. That Act shifts the focus for small businesses, defined as fewer than 20 workers, toward managing critical risks rather than treating every risk the same way.

Some business owners have read that as a loosening. It is not, and falls from height are exactly the wrong risk to relax on. If you work at height at all, falls are almost certainly one of your critical risks. Under the new framework that means it gets more of your attention, not less. The other change worth noting is that Approved Codes of Practice become a safe harbour, so where an ACOP covers a risk and you follow it, you are deemed to have complied for that risk. Following recognised guidance is about to carry real legal weight.

## What this means for your business

Three practical exposures, in order of how often we see them.

**Your documentation still says three metres.** If your health and safety plan, SWMS, or task analysis references a three metre trigger, it is now openly out of step with WorkSafe guidance. That is a finding waiting to happen in a SiteWise or Tōtika assessment, and it is a very unhelpful document to hand an investigator after an incident.

**Short duration work is treated as an exception.** Clearing a gutter, fixing a flashing, running a cable through a ceiling space. Short jobs are where controls quietly disappear because setting up feels disproportionate. WorkSafe has specific guidance on short duration work at height and the expectation is that you plan it, not that you skip it.

**Ladders are doing work they should not be doing.** A ladder is access equipment. When it becomes a work platform you have usually skipped a step in the hierarchy of controls.

## Concrete steps to take this month

Start by running a search through your safety documentation for "3m" and "three metres" and removing any wording that implies duties begin at a height. Replace it with the actual test: could a fall from this position injure someone, and what are we doing about it.

Next, list every task in your business that involves any risk of falling, including the low and quick ones. For each, work down the hierarchy before you land on PPE. Guardrails and edge protection first, then scaffolds and mobile elevating work platforms, then fall restraint, and harnesses only where nothing higher up the list is reasonably practicable. WorkSafe is clear that the people doing the work should help choose the controls, and that access, inspection, edge protection and rescue arrangements need to be agreed before work starts, not improvised on the day.

Then check your evidence. Pre-qualification schemes want to see the last three to six months of records: toolbox talks, equipment inspections, training, and completed corrective actions. Good controls with no paper trail score badly.

Finally, if you use subcontractors, ask what their height controls are and get it in writing. Overlapping duties do not transfer. If they fall on your site, you are in the conversation.

## The cost of doing nothing

Nothing happens for a long time, and then it does. A fall from two metres can be a fatality, a WorkSafe investigation, an ACC experience rating hit, a stalled project, and a pre-qualification score that quietly closes doors on tenders you used to win. Fixing the wording in a document and running one honest review of your height tasks costs a fraction of that.
